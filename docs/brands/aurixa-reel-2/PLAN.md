| Length   | 99.971 s; the speech ends at 99.78 s                                                                                               |

**It is Reel 1's voice.** Speaker embeddings were taken over 10-second windows (WeSpeaker ResNet34, trained on
VoxCeleb). Windows from the two recordings match each other as closely as windows from the same recording:

- Reel 2 against Reel 1's master: 0.915 on average (0.854 to 0.953);
- within Reel 1: 0.916;
- within Reel 2: 0.912.

The file is dated 13 October 2026, the day Reel 1's master was made
(`ElevenLabs_2026-10-13T09_15_32_Gabby-Soft-Young-Australian…wav`), and it pronounces "Aurixa" the way Reel 1 does.

**A question of quality.** Reel 1's master was ElevenLabs' own WAV. This copy is an MP3 that has been through an
encoder at least once. If ElevenLabs' own download of this voiceover is still in the account, use that instead.
Otherwise this file will do: 128 kbit/s holds speech well.

**The word times are provisional.** They come from two passes here:

- a recogniser run on this machine (NVIDIA's Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2, in sherpa-onnx) placed every word;
- each word's end was clipped to the pause measured in the audio (`voiceover-words.json`).

The studio's own preparation replaces these times before the brief is written. That needs the recording imported
(`import_audio`, as a voiceover), and the import asks the client to confirm the voice's consent (section 8,
decision 1).

## 2. The script, as the captions

The captions are the script's words. There is no written script yet, so the last column of the shot table (section 4)
is the voice as heard, written in the brand's style:

- the names as the product spells them: Aurixa Systems, AML/CTF, Compliance Passport;

`scripts/aurixa-reel-2/shot_times.py` checks those words against the voice, shot by shot, so a caption can't say
anything the voice doesn't. If there is a written script, send it, and the captions follow its punctuation.

## 3. The story

Reel 1 followed 14 Wattle Grove from an enquiry, through research, the financial analysis and the client's report, to
a recorded decision. Reel 2 tells the same purchase from the other side: what everyone working on it needs to know
about the client.

1. **The messenger** (light, unbranded). One property, six professionals, and every request for the same proof routed
   through the client, until the paperwork buries the property.
2. **One connected system** (the brand arrives). The logo leaps out on the music's drop, and the platform's parts dock
   into one Command Centre. Its AML/CTF Compliance entry opens.
3. **The Passport.** The bound document the product issues rises out of the dark, opens, and turns through the checks
   it records. Each stamp is struck as the voice names it.
4. **Shared, with consent.** The client consents in their own portal and the business sends the Passport to a partner.
   The partner opens the same document in their portal, in two clicks, and the requests from the opening fold into it.
5. **The result.** The site's own before and after: the chain of forms and follow-ups falls away, and the six
   professionals are wired to the Passport instead of to the client.
6. **The close** (shared with Reel 1). One part of Aurixa, then the living call to action, the four statements and the
   closing card.

Every Aurixa screen carries the Demo workspace label, and every value on it comes from the brand kit's demo record
(section 5.3).

## 4. The shots

- it sits in the pause before the shot's first word, 0.15 s ahead of the speech;

- either way, it falls on the 30 fps grid.

The times are on the voice's clock. They move a little once the studio measures the voice. Pacing the film to its music

The brief for Eleven Music (instrumental, 120 seconds):

> Instrumental, modern cinematic corporate at 120 BPM in 4/4. A sparse, curious intro of muted plucks and a soft ticking
> pulse that grows busier, light and never ominous. At 0:21 a warm drop: analogue pads, a confident groove, a shimmering
> arpeggio. A short build from 0:39 into a fuller, wider second drop at 0:42, assured rather than aggressive. At 1:10 a
> two-bar breakdown to pads, then the groove returns lighter. At 1:32 four stabs, one a bar, with space between. At 1:40
> the full return, ending on a final downbeat at 1:52 with a two-second ring. No vocals. Leave room for a spoken voice
> between 300 Hz and 4 kHz.

A prompt places its sections only roughly, so each take is read in the app for its bars and sections
(`get_music_structure`). The best one is then fitted by whole bars (`fit_music`). If no take lands well enough, the
next step is ElevenLabs' composition plan, which fixes each section's length. The studio sends only a prompt today,
so using a composition plan would need a small change to the studio.

## 7. Sound, pace and length

- **The voice is never stretched.** It is held open in its own silences, in whole frames, so that the cuts land on bars
  and beats. Reel 1's solver (`pacing.py`) does this. The holds that matter most come:
  - before the brand's drop, after "paperwork";
  - before "Introducing";
  - after the Passport lands;
  - after "The result?";
  - around the four statements;
  - before the closing card.
- **Length.** About 1:50 with those holds; the solver decides between 1:45 and 1:58. Reel 1 ran 2:11.
- **The mix.** It is done with the studio's mixer, as Reel 1's was (`soundtrack.ts`):
  - the music is ducked under speech section by section, but never in the breakdown;
  - the film is mastered to -14 LUFS, with a true peak of -1 dBTP or lower;
  - a mix report gives the voice's margin over the music in the speech band.

## 8. What the client decides

1. **The voice's consent statement.** The studio imports the recording only once the client confirms, in the studio's
   words: "Recorded by or for the brand, with the speaker's permission to use their voice in its content." Two things
   go with it:
   - It is Reel 1's ElevenLabs voice, so Reel 1's condition applies: the account's plan must cover commercial use.
   - If ElevenLabs' own download of this voiceover exists, send that instead of the WhatsApp copy.
2. **The music.** Make it in the studio (recommended, section 6), or name a licensed track.
3. **The responsibility line.** The script says the Passport supports "legislative compliance" while "eliminating
   unnecessary duplication", and that clients stop "providing the same information". The brand's guardrails ask that
   nothing says Aurixa makes a business compliant (guardrail 3), or that a shared Passport lets another business skip
   its own checks (guardrail 4).
   - The recommendation: put the brand's own line, "Your business remains responsible for its AML/CTF obligations.",
     under 05.3, small and calm, for about three seconds.
   - The script stays as it is.
4. **The captions:** the words in section 4's table, or the written script.
5. **The demo record's additions** in section 5.3: the names, the dates and the credential.
6. **The opening's six professionals,** as roles: buyer's agent, finance broker, solicitor or conveyancer, accountant,
   builder and developer.
7. **The free trial.** The script offers a 14-day free trial, as Reel 1 did. But the site's pricing page (in its code as of 13
   October) answers "Is there a free trial?" with a sandbox set up on request. Confirm that the 14-day trial is the
   offer (guardrail 9).
8. **The close.** Either Reel 1's closing card and logo footage, so the series ends the same way each time
   (recommended), or a new close for this reel, which needs the Higgsfield balance topped up.
